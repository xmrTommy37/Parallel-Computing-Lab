\section{Discussion}
Our experiments tell a coherent story that goes beyond the raw numbers. The Forest Fire automaton is an \emph{embarrassingly parallel} stencil in its computation phase, and this is confirmed by the near-ideal scaling of the computation-only time (Figure~\ref{fig:commcompute}): doubling the process count roughly halves the useful work. What limits the observed speedup is therefore not the algorithm but the interplay between the halo exchange and the hardware we ran on.

Three findings deserve emphasis. First, speedup improves with problem size (Table~\ref{tab:speedup}): the largest grid ($N=2000$) reaches $3.7\times$ at $p=4$ while the smallest ($N=500$) barely reaches $2\times$. This is the classic surface-to-volume argument---communication grows with the sub-domain perimeter while computation grows with its area, so bigger tiles are more favourable. Second, the $p=8$ regression is a resource-contention effect, not an algorithmic wall: with only 8 physical cores, running 8 MPI processes leaves nothing for the OS and saturates the shared memory bus, and the communication fraction jumps from $6\%$ to over $50\%$. Third, and perhaps most instructive, the 1D decomposition matched or beat the 2D one (Table~\ref{tab:topology}) despite its larger asymptotic message volume, because on a single shared-memory node message \emph{latency} and \emph{count} dominate over \emph{bandwidth}. The textbook superiority of 2D blocking is a property of large-scale distributed systems, not of an 8-core laptop.

\subsection{Limitations}
The most important caveat is that all experiments ran on a single shared-memory workstation, so the ``communication'' we measured is really memory-copy and synchronization cost, not network traffic. This inverts the usual cost model and is exactly why 2D blocking showed no advantage: on a real interconnect, where latency per message is orders of magnitude higher, the $\mathcal{O}(N/\sqrt{p})$ volume of the 2D scheme would matter far more. The weak-scaling points at $p=9$ and $p=16$ had to oversubscribe the cores, so they overstate the communication penalty and should be read as qualitative only. We also fixed the two probabilities and the initial density; a full study of the model's self-organized-critical regime (varying $p/l$ ratios and measuring fire-cluster statistics) is outside our scope, since our goal was performance rather than physics. Finally, our timers measure wall-clock time under one process placement policy; NUMA effects and OS scheduling introduce run-to-run noise that the three-repetition averaging only partially removes.

\subsection{Possible improvements}
The clearest next step is to overlap communication and computation using non-blocking primitives (\texttt{MPI\_Isend}/\texttt{MPI\_Irecv}): a process could post its halo exchange, update its \emph{interior} cells---which do not depend on ghost data---while the messages are in flight, and only then update the border cells once the transfers complete. This would hide most of the communication term that currently caps the speedup at $p=8$. Second, the halo could be exchanged with MPI derived datatypes (\texttt{MPI\_Type\_vector}) for the column borders instead of explicit \texttt{.copy()} buffers, removing the packing overhead. Third, a genuine assessment of the 2D-versus-1D trade-off requires a multi-node cluster, where the network cost model would finally favour the perimeter-minimizing 2D layout. Finally, for very large grids a hybrid MPI\,+\,OpenMP (or MPI\,+\,NumPy-threaded) scheme could exploit intra-node parallelism without paying the inter-process halo cost.
